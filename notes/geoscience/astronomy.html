    Big Bang

    The Big Bang is the prevailing cosmological model for the origin of the Universe. About 13.8 billion years ago, all of space, time, matter and energy expanded from an extremely hot, dense initial state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. The model is supported by three main lines of evidence: the expansion of the Universe (Hubble's law — distant galaxies recede faster), the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation, and the observed abundances of light elements (≈75% H, ≈25% He by mass) produced during Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

    Key epochs after the Big Bang (above): inflation (a brief phase of exponential expansion), formation of the first atoms at ~380,000 years (releasing the CMB), formation of the first stars and galaxies after a few hundred million years, and the accelerating expansion driven by dark energy in the present epoch.

    After the first stars formed, matter began to organize into stars and galaxies. Stars are not eternal — they evolve and die, and the path a star follows depends almost entirely on its mass.

    $$L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4$$

    The Hertzsprung–Russell (H–R) diagram (above) plots stellar luminosity (or absolute magnitude) against surface temperature. Most stars, including the Sun, lie on the diagonal main sequence, where they fuse hydrogen into helium. Cool, luminous red giants sit in the upper right, and hot, faint white dwarfs in the lower left. A star's position on this diagram tracks its evolutionary stage.

    The life cycle of a star (above) branches by mass. An average star like the Sun swells into a red giant, sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leaves behind a white dwarf. A massive star becomes a red supergiant and ends in a supernova, leaving a neutron star or, if massive enough, a black hole. The heavy elements forged in stars and dispersed by supernovae enrich the gas from which later stars and planets — including our Solar System — form.

    Solar System

    The Solar System (above, shown to relative scale) consists of the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, and countless small bodies (asteroids, comets, Kuiper Belt objects). The Sun contains about 99.86% of the total mass of the system, so its gravity governs the orbits of everything else. The four small, rocky terrestrial planets lie close to the Sun, while the four large giant planets orbit farther out.

    Sun

    The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star (G2V), a hot ball of plasma generating energy by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium in its core.

    • Radius: $6.96 \times 10^8 \text{ m}$ ($109$ times Earth)
    • Mass: $1.99 \times 10^{30} \text{ kg}$ ($3.3\times 10^5$ times Earth)
    • Composition: ~98% H and He (by mass, roughly 74% H, 24% He)
    • Main-sequence lifetime: ~10 billion years (currently ~4.6 billion years old, about halfway through)
    • Rotation period: ~25 Earth days at the equator (the Sun rotates differentially — slower near the poles, ~35 days)
    • Core temperature: ~$1.5 \times 10^7 \text{ K}$; surface (photosphere): ~6000 K
    • Energy generation: proton–proton chain fusion ($4\,^1\text{H} \rightarrow {}^4\text{He}$ + energy)
    • Solar activity: solar wind, sunspots, solar flares, coronal mass ejections — driven by the Sun's magnetic field and following an ~11-year cycle

    Layered structure of the Sun (above), with temperatures in kelvin. From the inside out: the core (~16,000,000 K, where fusion occurs), the photosphere (~6000 K, the visible "surface"), and the tenuous outer atmosphere — the chromosphere (~10,000 K) and corona (~1,000,000 K). Counter-intuitively, the corona is far hotter than the surface, a long-standing puzzle ("coronal heating problem"). Prominences and flares are eruptions of magnetized plasma above the surface.

    Planet

    Definition of a planet (2006, IAU): a body that (1) orbits the Sun, (2) has enough mass to achieve hydrostatic equilibrium and is nearly spherical, and (3) has cleared other bodies from the neighbourhood of its orbit. Bodies meeting (1) and (2) but not (3) — such as Pluto and Ceres — are classified as dwarf planets.

    We can divide the eight planets into: Terrestrial: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars Gas Giants: Jupiter, Saturn Ice Giants: Uranus, Neptune

    The terrestrial planets are small, dense, and rocky with solid surfaces; the gas giants are huge and dominated by H/He; the ice giants are smaller giants richer in "ices" (water, ammonia, methane).

    Parameters used to characterize a planet:

    1. mass, size, shape, density, etc.
    2. host star, space environment, magnetic field, etc.
    3. orbit, revolution, rotation, axial tilt, etc.
    4. atmosphere, temperature, pressure, circulation, etc.
    5. surface, ocean and continent, interior, evolution, etc.

    The planets compared (above), with axial tilt ($\theta$) and rotation period for each. Note the extremes: Venus rotates retrograde (upside-down, $\theta \approx 177^\circ$) and very slowly (243 days), while Uranus is tipped on its side ($\theta \approx 98^\circ$), effectively "rolling" along its orbit.

    Planetary magnetic fields (above). A global magnetic field requires a convecting, electrically conducting interior (a dynamo). Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune have strong fields; their magnetic and rotation axes are often misaligned (dramatically so for Uranus and Neptune). A planet's magnetosphere shields its atmosphere from the solar wind.

    The atmospheres of the planets differ markedly in composition. The terrestrial atmospheres (below) range from Earth's N₂/O₂ mix to the CO₂-dominated atmospheres of Venus and Mars; Titan (Saturn's moon) has a thick N₂ atmosphere.

    The giant planets (below) are dominated by hydrogen and helium, with trace methane and ammonia that give them their colours.

    Mercury

    • the smallest planet in the Solar System; closest to the Sun
    • nearly no atmosphere (only a thin, transient exosphere)
    • heavily cratered surface, much like the Moon
    • has smooth plains and long cliffs (lobate scarps) formed as the planet cooled and contracted
    • no natural satellite
    • has a weak dipole magnetic field, surprising for such a small, slowly rotating planet — evidence of a partly molten iron core
    • very large iron core relative to its size; extreme day–night temperature range

    Venus

    Venus's atmosphere (above): the surface is ~730 K under a crushing ~90 bar CO₂ atmosphere with sulfuric-acid clouds — a runaway greenhouse effect makes it the hottest planet despite Mercury being closer to the Sun.

    • no natural satellite
    • nearly Earth's size and mass ("Earth's twin"), but has essentially no water
    • many extinct volcanoes; may also have active volcanoes
    • virtually no intrinsic magnetic field, because of insufficient core convection (slow rotation)
    • has an induced magnetosphere, formed by the interaction between the solar wind and the ionosphere of Venus's upper atmosphere
    • rotates retrograde and extremely slowly (one Venusian day > one Venusian year)

    Mars

    • very thin atmosphere (~0.6% of Earth's surface pressure), mostly CO₂
    • no stable liquid water at the surface today
    • two natural satellites: Phobos and Deimos (small, irregular, likely captured asteroids)
    • weak, localized crustal magnetism only — no global field today (its dynamo shut down early)
    • soil contains $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ (iron oxide), giving the "Red Planet" its colour
    • polar caps contain dry ice (CO₂) and water ice
    • surface features include sandstone, sand dunes, extinct volcanoes (e.g. Olympus Mons, the largest in the Solar System) and ancient river valleys
    • abundant evidence (channels, deltas, minerals) that liquid water once flowed — Mars may have had oceans in the past

    Jupiter

    • the largest planet; 90+ known natural satellites, including the four large Galilean moons (Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto)
    • no solid surface — a giant ball of H/He
    • numerous storms and vortices, including the Great Red Spot and polar cyclones
    • contains a large amount of liquid metallic hydrogen deep in its interior, which drives a powerful magnetic field
    • hot interior; radiates more energy than it receives from the Sun

    Saturn

    • 140+ natural satellites (e.g. Enceladus, with subsurface ocean geysers, and Titan, with a thick atmosphere and liquid-methane lakes)
    • spectacular ring system composed of water ice and rock grains
    • has lightning and giant thunderstorms
    • a distinctive hexagonal jet-stream pattern around its north pole, plus vortices
    • lowest mean density of any planet (less dense than water)

    Saturn and its rings, imaged by the Cassini spacecraft (above).

    Uranus

    • 27+ natural satellites
    • atmosphere contains methane, which absorbs red light and gives the planet its cyan colour
    • haze in the upper atmosphere mutes its appearance (whitish/pale in visible light)
    • extreme axial tilt (~98°) — it orbits "on its side," producing decades-long seasons
    • coldest planetary atmosphere in the Solar System

    Neptune

    • 16+ natural satellites (largest is Triton, which orbits retrograde)
    • atmosphere contains methane, giving a deep blue colour
    • the windiest planet — maximum wind speeds reach ~$2100 \text{ km/h}$ (over $500\ \text{m/s}$; some estimates exceed $300\ \text{m/s}$ sustained)
    • like Jupiter, radiates more heat than it receives from the Sun

    Small Celestial Bodies

    Besides the planets, the Solar System contains a vast population of small bodies: asteroids (mostly in the Main Belt between Mars and Jupiter), comets, Trojans (sharing a planet's orbit), Centaurs, and the Kuiper Belt Objects beyond Neptune (including dwarf planets like Pluto and Eris). Far beyond lies the hypothesized Oort Cloud, source of long-period comets.

    Size versus distance from the Sun for Solar System bodies (above). The planets (squares and diamonds) are far larger than the swarms of small bodies. The plot reveals the dynamical structure: the Main-Belt Asteroids (~2–3 AU), the Jupiter Trojans, and the Kuiper Belt Objects (~30+ AU). These small bodies are leftover planetesimals from the era of planet formation and preserve a record of the early Solar System.

    Formation of Solar System

    New stars in the Milky Way consist of ~98% H and He with ~2% heavier elements. The Solar System began as an interstellar cloud of gas and dust — the Solar Nebula. A disturbance (perhaps a nearby supernova) triggered its gravitational collapse; conservation of angular momentum (see Angular Momentum (TODO)) flattened it into a rotating protoplanetary disk with the proto-Sun at the center. As particles collided and the central mass grew, the young Sun entered the T-Tauri phase.

    Early Sun:

    • lasts ~100 million years
    • H burning has not yet begun (still contracting)
    • heated as it contracts and grows
    • emits intense X-rays, radio waves, and a strong solar wind
    • loses ~50% of its mass early on
    • the solar wind blows away residual gases and volatiles
    • large radius
    • about half of such young stars have surrounding disks (now directly imaged around other stars)

    Solar Nebula Condensation Theory explains where different materials form. Temperature in the disk fell with distance from the Sun, so different materials condensed at different radii. This is why the inner planets are rocky/metallic and the outer planets are rich in ices and gas.

    The condensation sequence (above): as the gas cools, refractory materials (oxides, metals) condense first at high temperature, then silicates, then sulfides and oxides (FeS, FeO), and finally — only in the cold outer disk, beyond the "frost line" — water ice and other volatile ices. The frost line marks the boundary between the rocky inner Solar System and the ice-rich outer Solar System.

    How do planets accrete? This is one of the open problems in current theories of planet formation.

    In 1972, Safronov's Planetesimal Theory was proposed. It explained how kilometre-sized planetesimals grow into planets through successive collisions and gravitational accretion. It remains the foundation of the modern planetary-formation model.

    Evidence for how the planets accreted:

    • Earliest records on the Moon, Mars and Mercury: intense cratering ("Late Heavy Bombardment")
    • As planets grow, their gravity increases, so impacts become more violent
    • Debris from impacts buries hot rock from earlier impacts
    • Heat builds up from impacts and from the decay of radioactive elements
    • Surfaces melt to form a magma ocean, allowing dense iron to sink and form a core (differentiation)

    The classic Safronov model has some difficulties:

    • Metre barrier: Safronov assumed dust could settle and grow smoothly. Modern research finds that objects from metres to kilometres in size experience strong gas drag ("headwind") in the disk, causing them to spiral into the star within a few hundred years before they can grow larger.
    • Gas giants: The model struggles to explain giants like Jupiter and Saturn, because collisions alone cannot build a solid core ~10 times Earth's mass within the few million years before the gas disk dissipates.
    • Planetary migration: Safronov assumed planets formed in situ in their current orbits. But exoplanet discoveries show that planets exchange angular momentum with the surrounding gas/dust during formation, producing substantial orbital migration (e.g. "hot Jupiters" found very close to their stars).

    To solve these problems, scientists propose Streaming Instability (dust concentrates and gravitationally collapses directly into planetesimals, bypassing the metre barrier) and Pebble Accretion (cores grow rapidly by sweeping up cm-sized pebbles, fast enough to form giant-planet cores before the gas disperses).

    Formation of Moon

    The leading explanation for the Moon's origin is the Giant Impact Hypothesis: early in Solar System history, a Mars-sized protoplanet (named Theia) collided with the proto-Earth. The debris ejected into orbit coalesced to form the Moon. This neatly explains the Moon's low iron content (Theia's and Earth's metal cores merged into Earth, leaving the debris disk silicate-rich), the Earth–Moon angular momentum, and the similarity of their isotopic compositions.

    Timeline of Solar System and Moon formation (above), from the condensation of the first solids (CAIs) and the growth of planetesimals, through the Moon-forming giant impact at ~50–100 Myr, to the differentiated Earth–Moon system with a possible "late veneer" of material delivered afterward.
