    Earth's Age

    What is the age of the Earth? This is an important problem in geoscience. The first answer based on geoscience was made by Lord Kelvin, who estimated that Earth's age ranged from 400 million to 20 million years. He assumed that its origin was by cooling from a molten state.

    In 1894, Henri Becquerel reported evidence for radioactivity by showing that photographic film became exposed when adjacent to uranium minerals. Then, Marie Curie and Pierre Curie identified two new radioactive elements – radium and polonium, which came to be recognized as intermediate elements formed from the radioactive decay of uranium.

    In 1907, Rutherford suggested that radioactive decay in uranium-bearing minerals could be used to date the crystallization age of the mineral if the rate of decay was known.

    Composition in Different Depth

    Formation of Continental Crust

    Partial melts generated in the mantle wedge rise either as diapirs of melt and crystals, which increasingly melt and become more basic as they rise and decompress, or as rapidly moving segregated melt. The upwelling melt cools by up to 1 °C/km (in contrast to 0.5 °C/km in solid mantle). At the base of the continental crust, lighter liquids can pass straight upwards to the surface. But most liquids are probably trapped by their density, because the continental crust may be less dense than the magma.

    Hot magma collecting at the base of the crust loses heat to the overlying continent and cools and fractionates, precipitating minerals such as clinopyroxene, together with garnet, olivine, or orthopyroxene. After fractionation, the lighter liquids rise to the surface, most probably as basalt and basaltic andesite magmas. After partial melting, the residual material left behind in the deep continental crust is granulite. It is depleted of all heating-producing elements, such as U, Th, and K, which are moved to the shallow level.

    Composition and Mineralogy of the Lower Mantle

    Almost all direct observations of deep mantle come from seismology. Any mineralogical model has to match the seismic observations.

    Small-scale heterogeneities (Scatterers):

    • Thickness is several or tens of kilometers
    • Velocity perturbation is 0.1–1% (throughout the mantle)
    • Shear velocity could be up to ~12% lower than the ambient mantle in local regions (Mariana and Peru)

    Large-scale heterogeneities:

    • Low-velocity columns beneath prominent hotspots
    • Positive velocity anomalies near subduction zones
    • Large low shear velocity provinces(LLSVPs)

    A pyrolitic composition along the normal geotherm can well reproduce the reference velocities and densities for the upper mantle.

    Mid-Ocean Ridge Basalts (MORB)

    As the subduction zone sinks into the oceanic crust deep into the mantle, it undergoes partial melting during the formation of the mid-ocean ridge. Its composition is rich in Si, Ca, and Al, resulting in a high-pressure mineral assemblage completely different from the surrounding normal mantle.

    Driving Force of Plate Tectonics

    Slab Pull

    Slab Pull is widely considered the primary force driving plate movement. This is because, over time, rocks formed from mid-ocean ridges are continuously pushed outwards, their temperature decreases, and their density increases. When this high-density oceanic plate collides with another plate, it bends under its own weight and subducts into the mantle. This force is also transmitted to the plate that has not yet subducted, pulling it along as well.

    During the subduction process, the increasing pressure on the rocks leads to phase transitions. For example, at depths of about 40-60 kilometers, basalt in the oceanic crust transforms into eclogite. Phase transitions are a significant reason for the increased density in subduction zones.

    As shown in the figure, we assume that the tectonic plate inserts into the mantle at an angle $\Psi$, and denote the density of the lithosphere as $\rho_L$ and the density of the mantle as $\rho_M$. Since the plate density gradually changes from $\rho_L$ to $\rho_M$ during subduction, we denote the density difference as $\frac{1}{2}(\rho_L-\rho_M)$. Let $E$ be the depth at which the density difference disappears during subduction, and $L$ be the thickness of the lithosphere. Because subduction actually involves a certain inclination, the actual extension length of the plate in the mantle (along the slope) is $E / \sin \Psi$. Therefore, the tensile force exerted by the plate on the subduction zone is...$$F_{sp}=\frac{\rho_L-\rho_M}{2\sin \Psi}ELg$$

    Ridge Push

    Mid-ocean ridge thrust is considered the second strongest force driving plate movement after plate tension. Its formation is generally attributed to two main factors:

    1. Due to the thinner lithosphere and higher elevation at mid-ocean ridges, the entire plate "slips" from the ridge to the sides under gravity, generating a horizontal pressure gradient force.
    2. Over time, newly formed plates gradually harden and age, increasing their density and beginning to sink, causing seafloor depth to increase with distance from the mid-ocean ridge.

    We can quantitatively analyze this physical process. First, from the one-dimensional heat conduction equation, we have$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\kappa \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}$$We assume:

    • The surface temperature is constant, i.e., $T(z=0,t)=T_0$
    • The temperature in the deep mantle is constant, i.e., $T(z=+\infty,t)=T_m$
    • We assume the lithosphere "floats" on the asthenosphere and that the pressure is equal at a certain compensation depth.

    The solution is:$$\frac{T(z, t) - T_0}{T_m - T_0} = \text{erf}\left( \frac{z}{2\sqrt{\kappa t}} \right)$$

    Therefore, for two points with the same temperature, $\frac{z}{2\sqrt{\kappa t}}$ is constant, meaning that the later the formation time, the thicker the plate.

    Considering the coefficient of thermal expansion $\alpha_v$, density distribution:$$\rho(z, t) = \rho_m [1 + \alpha_v (T_m - T(z, t))]$$ Substituting the temperature formula into:$$\rho(z, t) = \rho_m \left[ 1 + \alpha_v (T_m-T_0) \left( 1 - \text{erf}\left( \frac{z}{2\sqrt{\kappa t}} \right) \right) \right]$$ The thrust of the mid-ocean ridge, $F_{rp}$, is the resultant force of the pressure difference between the two locations over the entire depth. $$\begin{align*}F_{rp} &= \int_0^\infty (P_{plate}(z, t) - P_{ridge}(z)) \, \mathrm{d}z\\&=g \rho_m \alpha_v (T_m-T_0) \int_0^\infty \left[ \int_z^\infty \text{erfc}\left( \frac{z'}{2\sqrt{\kappa t}} \right) \mathrm{d}z' \right] \mathrm{d}z\\= &g \rho_m \alpha_v (T_m - T_0) \left[ 1 + \frac{2}{\pi} \frac{\rho_m \alpha_v (T_m - T_0)}{\rho_m - \rho_0} \right] \kappa t\end{align*}$$

    Morphology and Fate of Subducted Slabs

    Subduction zones

    The Wadati-Benioff region is a deep, tilted zone of intense seismic activity within the Earth's interior.

    Earthquakes in the Benioff Belt are generally classified into three categories based on depth:

    • Shallow-focus earthquakes (0-70 km): Primarily occur at the frictional interface between subducting and overlying plates, exhibiting extremely high destructive power and easily triggering tsunamis.
    • Intermediate-focus earthquakes (70-300 km): Occur within the subducting plate, mainly caused by plate bending and dehydration embrittlement.
    • Deep-focus earthquakes (300-700 km): Extremely deep, generally believed to be caused by volumetric collapse resulting from abrupt changes in mineral structure (such as the transformation of olivine into spinel).

    Ultimate depth (approximately 660-700 km): Seismic activity in the Benioff Belt typically disappears abruptly at around 700 km. This is because at this depth, the plate is finally fully heated and softened, or encounters the denser lower mantle, no longer possessing the conditions for brittle fracture.

    It is noted that the cause of deep-focus earthquakes (>300km) remains a mystery, because at this depth, rocks should exhibit plasticity under high temperature and pressure and should not undergo brittle fracture.

    Why does plate tectonics only occur on the Earth's surface?

    • Surface liquid water. When water penetrates deep into the lithosphere, it significantly lowers the melting point and physical strength of silicate rocks. Water molecules enter the mineral lattice, making the normally extremely hard lithosphere more ductile, thus allowing faults to form and plates to break apart.
    • Just the right surface temperature. The temperature of a planet's surface directly determines the mechanical properties of the lithosphere; it must be within a temperature range that allows rocks to exhibit brittleness (the ability to fracture and form plates).
    • Mantle convection. The Earth's large size and mass contain an immense amount of heat. This heat causes slow and persistent thermal convection in the mantle. Hot material rises to form mid-ocean ridges, while cooler material sinks to form subduction zones.

    Earth Evolution

    1. Hadean and Early Archean Eon (approximately 4.5-3 billion years ago): The Insane "Non-Plate" Era

    Early Earth was much hotter than it is today (mantle temperatures may have been 200-300°C higher), primarily due to the accretion of heat from numerous meteorites and high concentrations of radioactive decay.

    Heat-pipe Tectonics: The extremely hot mantle resulted in a very thin, magnesium-rich crust, resembling a soft, "pasty" layer. At this time, the crust had high buoyancy and could not sink into the mantle. The primary way Earth dissipated heat was not through horizontal plate movement, but through countless magma columns erupting vertically from deep within the mantle, like "chimneys," penetrating the crust.

    Drip Tectonics: As localized areas of the crust cooled and became heavier, they did not slide or subduct laterally like plates today, but instead "dripped" or sank directly into the mantle, like water droplets from a ceiling.

    1. Late Archean to Paleoproterozoic (approximately 3-2 billion years ago): The Pains and Initiation of Plate Tectonics

    This was a crucial transitional period, with the Earth cooling to a critical point.

    Episodic Subduction: Decreasing mantle temperatures caused the lithosphere to become thicker, denser, and more rigid. Occasionally, fragments of cooled lithosphere would begin to sink into the mantle, marking the beginning of subduction.

    Fragile Plates: However, the mantle was still relatively warm at this time, and the subducting plates would quickly soften and fracture, often breaking off shortly after submersion. Therefore, early plate tectonics was localized, intermittent, and sporadic, lacking a globally interconnected network. Liquid water also played a crucial "lubricant" role during this stage, facilitating fault activity.

    1. Proterozoic to Present (Approximately 2 billion years ago to present): Modern Plate Tectonics and the Supercontinent Cycle

    As mantle temperatures further decreased and the lithosphere became sufficiently hard and heavy, global modern plate tectonics were finally fully established.

    The Wilson Cycle Dominates: The Earth's crust began to fracture regularly, seafloor spreading, oceans closing, and continental collisions creating mountains. Deep and continuous subduction zones (the Vadati-Benioff zones) formed globally.

    The Supercontinent Cycle: The horizontal movement of plates causes continental blocks on Earth to assemble into a "supercontinent" approximately every 400 to 600 million years, only to break apart again. Famous supercontinents in geological history include: Nuna/Columbia, Rodinia, and the most recent supercontinent, Pangea, which formed approximately 250 million years ago.

    Wilson Cycle

    The Wilson cycle is a model that describes the opening and closing of ocean basins and the subduction and divergence of tectonic plates during the assembly and disassembly of supercontinents.

    Snowball Incident

    Snowball Earth is a fascinating and awe-inspiring hypothesis in geology and paleoclimatology. It refers to one or more extreme global glacial events that occurred during Earth's long history. During these periods, the Earth's surface—from the poles to the equator—was almost entirely or completely covered by thick ice and snow. From space, the Earth at that time resembled a giant, dazzling "snowball."

    Earth's history has seen more than one such extreme ice age, the two most famous and well-documented occurring during the Neoproterozoic Era, approximately 720 million to 635 million years ago, known as the Sturtian and Marinoan Glaciations.
