    Groups

    A group is a set $G$ with a binary operation $\cdot$ satisfying:

    1. Associativity: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$;
    2. Identity: $\exists\, 1 \in G$ with $a \cdot 1 = 1 \cdot a = a$;
    3. Inverse: $\forall a$, $\exists\, a^{-1}$ with $a a^{-1} = a^{-1} a = 1$.

    The number of elements $|G|$ is the order. The identity and inverses are unique, and cancellation holds: $ab = ac \Rightarrow b = c$.

    Structures with fewer axioms: semigroup (1), monoid (1)+(2), group (1)–(3); adding commutativity $ab = ba$ gives an Abelian group. (In a monoid, if a left and right identity both exist they coincide; same for left/right inverses.)

    Direct product. If $G_1, \dots, G_k$ are groups, the Cartesian product $G = G_1 \times \cdots \times G_k$ with componentwise operation $(g_1, \dots, g_k)(h_1, \dots, h_k) = (g_1 h_1, \dots, g_k h_k)$ is a group.

    A nonempty subset $H \subseteq G$ closed under the operation and inverses is a subgroup, $H \le G$.

    Homomorphisms

    A map $f : G \to G'$ is a group homomorphism if $f(g k) = f(g) f(k)$. If $f$ is bijective it is an isomorphism ($G \cong G'$); injective = monomorphism; surjective = epimorphism. Then $f(1) = 1'$ and $f(g^{-1}) = f(g)^{-1}$. An isomorphism $G \to G$ is an automorphism; all automorphisms form $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$.

    • Center: $Z(G) = \{z \in G \mid zg = gz\ \forall g\}$.
    • Normal subgroup: $H \trianglelefteq G$ if $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$. Every subgroup of an Abelian group is normal, and $Z(G) \trianglelefteq G$.
    • Simple group: $G$ has no normal subgroups other than $\{1\}$ and $G$.
    • Conjugate subgroups: $H, K$ with $K = gHg^{-1}$ for some $g$; then $H \cong K$.
    • Normalizer: $N_G(H) = \{x \in G \mid xH = Hx\}$. Then $N_G(H) \le G$, $H \trianglelefteq N_G(H)$, and $N_G(H)$ is the largest subgroup in which $H$ is normal.
    • Kernel and image: $\ker f = f^{-1}(1')$ and $\operatorname{im} f = \{f(g)\}$; $\ker f \trianglelefteq G$ and $\operatorname{im} f \le H$.

    Cyclic Groups

    For $g \in G$, $\langle g \rangle = \{g^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$. If $G = \langle g \rangle$, $G$ is cyclic with generator $g$. If $g$ has finite order $k$, then $g^n = 1 \iff k \mid n$ and $g^i = g^j \iff i \equiv j \pmod k$; the map $\mathbb{Z} \to G$, $n \mapsto g^n$ is a surjective homomorphism.

    For cyclic $G$: if infinite, $G \cong \mathbb{Z}$; if order $n$, the generators are $\{g^k \mid 0 \le k < n,\ (k, n) = 1\}$, so there are $\varphi(n)$ of them. A homomorphic image of a cyclic group is cyclic.

    Cosets and Lagrange's Theorem

    For $H \le G$, $a \in G$, the left coset is $aH = \{ah \mid h \in H\}$. Two cosets are equal or disjoint, so $G = \bigsqcup_a aH$. Also $aH = bH \iff a^{-1}b \in H$, and $aH = Ha \iff aHa^{-1} = H$, i.e. $H \trianglelefteq G \iff gH = Hg\ \forall g$.

    The map $aH \to bH$, $ah \mapsto bh$ is a bijection, so $|aH| = |H|$. Hence $|G| = [G : H]\,|H|$.

    TLagrange

    The order of any subgroup of a finite group divides the order of the group.

    • Corollary 1. $\forall x \in G$, $x^{|G|} = 1$.
    • Corollary 2. A group of prime order is cyclic and simple.

    Quotient Groups

    For $H \trianglelefteq G$, the set of cosets $G/H = \{gH\}$ with $[aH][bH] = [ab]H$ is a well-defined group (well-definedness requires $H$ normal). By Lagrange, $|G/H| = [G : H] = |G|/|H|$. If $[G : H] = 2$ then $g^2 \in H$ for all $g$.

    TFirst Isomorphism Theorem (groups)

    If $f : G \to H$ is a surjective homomorphism, then $G / \ker f \cong \operatorname{im} f$.

    Symmetric Groups

    A bijection of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ is a permutation $\sigma$; they form the symmetric group $S_n$ with $|S_n| = n!$ (non-Abelian for $n > 2$). A $k$-cycle $(i_1 i_2 \cdots i_k)$ sends $i_1 \to i_2 \to \cdots \to i_k \to i_1$ and fixes the rest; a $2$-cycle is a transposition. Disjoint cycles commute, and every permutation factors uniquely (up to order) into disjoint cycles.

    $S_n$ is generated by the transpositions $(12), (13), \dots, (1n)$. The sign $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = (-1)^{n - \#\text{cycles}}$ is a homomorphism $S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$, with $\operatorname{sgn}$ of a $k$-cycle $= (-1)^{k-1}$. Even permutations (sign $+1$) form the alternating group $A_n$, and the even/odd permutations are equinumerous.

    A group of order $p^n$ ($p$ prime) is a $p$-group.

    TCayley

    A group $G$ of order $n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_n$. Proof. For $a \in G$, $f_a : g \mapsto ag$ is a bijection, so $f_a \in S_n$; $a \mapsto f_a$ is an injective homomorphism since $\ker = \{a : ag = g\ \forall g\} = \{1\}$. $\square$

    Group Actions

    Let $G$ act on a set $S$. The orbit $\operatorname{Orb}(s) = \{s \cdot g \mid g \in G\}$ and stabilizer $\operatorname{Stab}(s) = \{g \mid g \cdot s = s\}$. Fixed points form $S^G$.

    TOrbit–Stabilizer

    $|\operatorname{Orb}(s)| \cdot |\operatorname{Stab}(s)| = |G|$.

    For a $p$-group, every orbit size is a power of $p$, so $|S^G| \equiv |S| \pmod p$.

    TCauchy

    If a prime $p$ divides $|G|$ (finite), then $G$ has an element of order $p$. Proof. Let $S = \{(x_1, \dots, x_p) \mid x_1 \cdots x_p = 1\}$, $|S| = |G|^{p-1}$. The cyclic shift $\sigma = (12\cdots p)$ acts on $S$; $|S^{\langle\sigma\rangle}| \equiv |S| \equiv 0 \pmod p$. Since $(1, \dots, 1)$ is fixed, there is another fixed point, giving $g^p = 1$. $\square$

    Class equation. $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum [G : N_G(g)]$ (sum over non-central conjugacy class representatives). For a $p$-group, $|Z(G)| \equiv 0 \pmod p$.

    TSylow (first theorem)

    If $|G| = p^n m$ with $p$ prime and $p \nmid m$, then for each $0 \le i \le n$, $G$ has a subgroup of order $p^i$ (a Sylow $p$-subgroup for $i = n$), and every $p$-subgroup is contained in some Sylow $p$-subgroup.

    Structural Consequences

    • If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is Abelian. (Proof: write $g = z^a g_0$; any two elements commute.)
    • Every group of order $p^2$ is Abelian.
    TInternal Direct Product Criterion

    If $H \trianglelefteq G$, $K \trianglelefteq G$, $H \cap K = \{1\}$, and $HK = G$, then $G \cong H \times K$ via $(h, k) \mapsto hk$.

    Some Common Groups

    • Dihedral group $D_n$: $n$ rotations $1, r, \dots, r^{n-1}$ and reflections, with $srs = r^{-1}$; non-Abelian for $n \ge 3$. Note $D_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$.
    • Non-Abelian group of order $2p$ ($p$ odd prime) $\cong D_p$.
    • Quaternion group $Q_n = \langle a, b \mid a^{2n} = 1,\ b^2 = a^n,\ bab^{-1} = a^{-1}\rangle$. For $n = 1$, $Q_1 = \{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$, $ijk = -1$; non-Abelian.
    TTheorem

    $A_n$ is simple for $n \ge 5$, but $A_4$ is not simple (since the Klein four-group $K_4 \trianglelefteq A_4$).

    Rings, Integral Domains, Fields

    In a ring, $x \cdot 0 = 0 \cdot x = 0$. An element $a \neq 0$ is a zero divisor if $ab = 0$ for some $b \neq 0$. A ring without zero divisors is an integral domain; a commutative ring in which every nonzero element is invertible is a field.

    TTheorem

    Every finite integral domain is a field. Proof. For $a \neq 0$, the map $x \mapsto ax$ is injective (no zero divisors), hence surjective on the finite set, so some $ax = 1$. $\square$

    Note: the direct product of nonzero integral domains is not an integral domain, since $(0, x)(y, 0) = (0, 0)$.

    A ring homomorphism $f : R \to R'$ satisfies $f(1) = 1'$, $f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b)$, $f(ab) = f(a)f(b)$; bijective gives a ring isomorphism. Then $f(0) = 0$, $f(-a) = -f(a)$.

    A subring $S \subseteq R$ is closed under $+, -, \times$ and contains $1$. An ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ is an additive subgroup with $rx \in I$ for all $r \in R$, $x \in I$ (e.g. $xR$ is a right ideal).

    Ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are principal: every ideal $I \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ equals $d\mathbb{Z}$ for the smallest positive $d \in I$. The kernel $\ker f = f^{-1}(0')$ of a ring homomorphism is an ideal, and $\operatorname{im} f$ is a subring.

    For an ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, the quotient ring $R/I$ has elements $r + I = \{r + i \mid i \in I\}$ with $$(r_1 + I) + (r_2 + I) = (r_1 + r_2) + I, \qquad (r_1 + I)(r_2 + I) = r_1 r_2 + I$$ (multiplication well-defined precisely because $I$ is an ideal).

    TFirst Isomorphism Theorem (rings)

    If $f : R \to R'$ is a surjective ring homomorphism, then $R / \ker f \cong \operatorname{im} f$.

    Polynomials

    For a field $F$, the polynomial ring $F[x] = \{a_n x^n + \cdots + a_1 x + a_0 \mid a_i \in F\}$, with $\deg f = n$.

    Division algorithm. For $f, g \in F[x]$ with $g \neq 0$, there exist unique $q, r \in F[x]$ with $f = qg + r$, $\deg r < \deg g$.

    gcd of polynomials is defined analogously; $F[x]$ ideals are principal, so for $f, g$ with $d = (f, g)$: $$\{f u + g v \mid u, v \in F[x]\} = \{d w \mid w \in F[x]\}$$ In particular $\exists\, u, v$ with $fu + gv = d = (f, g)$, and $f, g$ coprime $\iff \exists\, u, v$ with $fu + gv = 1$. Also $(f, g) = 1$ and $(f, h) = 1 \Rightarrow (f, gh) = 1$.

    A nonconstant $f \in F[x]$ is irreducible if it cannot be factored into lower-degree polynomials (its only divisors are constants and $cf$). Euclid's lemma and unique factorization hold for $F[x]$ as for $\mathbb{Z}$: $f = c\, p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_r^{e_r}$ uniquely, with gcd/lcm given by min/max of exponents.

    TRemainder / Factor Theorem

    $f(x) = q(x)(x - a) + f(a)$. Hence $f(a) = 0 \iff (x - a) \mid f(x)$ ($a$ is a root).

    TLagrange

    A polynomial of degree $n$ over a field has at most $n$ roots.

    TVieta's Formulas

    If $f(x) = a_n(x - x_1)\cdots(x - x_n)$, then the elementary symmetric functions satisfy $$\sum_{i_1 < \cdots < i_k} x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k} = (-1)^k \frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}$$

    Over $\mathbb{F}_p$: $x^p - x = \prod_{a \in \mathbb{F}_p}(x - a)$, whence $x^{p-1} - 1 \equiv (x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-p+1) \pmod p$, giving Wilson's theorem $(p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$.

    TWolstenholme

    For a prime $p > 5$, $\;\dbinom{2p}{p} \equiv 2 \pmod{p^3}$ (equivalently the symmetric functions $S_1, S_{p-2}$ of $1, \dots, p-1$ vanish $\bmod p^2$).

    Polynomial congruences. $f \equiv g \pmod{m(x)}$ means $m(x) \mid (f - g)$; congruences add and multiply as usual. The quotient $F[x]/(m(x))$ has representatives $\{[r(x)] : \deg r < \deg m\}$ with units $\{[a(x)] : (a, m) = 1\}$, and $$F[x]/(m(x)) \text{ is a field} \iff m(x) \text{ is irreducible}$$ For $m = m_1 \cdots m_r$ with $m_i$ pairwise coprime, the polynomial CRT gives $F[x]/(m) \cong \prod_i F[x]/(m_i)$.

    Irreducibility.

    • Over $\mathbb{C}$: $f$ irreducible $\iff \deg f = 1$ (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).
    • Over $\mathbb{R}$: irreducible $\iff$ degree $1$, or degree $2$ with $\Delta < 0$.
    TGauss's Lemma

    If $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ factors over $\mathbb{Q}$, then it factors over $\mathbb{Z}$.

    TEisenstein's Criterion

    Let $f(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, $a_n \neq 0$. If a prime $p$ satisfies $p \mid a_i$ for $i = 0, \dots, n-1$, $p \nmid a_n$, and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

    Mod $p$ reduction. If $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is irreducible mod $p$, it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$ (contrapositive: reducible over $\mathbb{Z} \Rightarrow$ reducible over $\mathbb{F}_p$).

    Symmetric Polynomials

    $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is symmetric if $f(x_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, x_{\sigma(n)}) = f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ for all $\sigma \in S_n$.

    TFundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials

    Every symmetric polynomial is a polynomial in the elementary symmetric polynomials $t_1, \dots, t_n$, and this expression is unique (the $t_i$ are algebraically independent).

    The discriminant of $f(x) = (x - X_1)\cdots(x - X_n)$ is $D_f = \prod_{i < j}(X_i - X_j)^2$, a symmetric polynomial of degree $n(n-1)$. For $f = x^2 + bx + c$: $D_f = b^2 - 4c$.

    TNewton's Identities

    With power sums $t_k = \sum_i X_i^k$ and elementary symmetric $S_j$:

    • For $k \le n - 1$: $\;t_k - S_1 t_{k-1} + \cdots + (-1)^{k-1}S_{k-1}t_1 + (-1)^k k\, S_k = 0$.
    • For $k \ge n$: $\;t_k - S_1 t_{k-1} + \cdots + (-1)^n S_n t_{k-n} = 0$.

    Cyclotomic Polynomials

    $$\Phi_n(x) = \prod_{\substack{1 \le k \le n \\ (k, n) = 1}} (x - \zeta_n^k), \qquad \zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$$ Properties. $\Phi_n$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and $x^n - 1 = \prod_{d \mid n}\Phi_d(x)$. For prime $p$, $\Phi_p(x) = \frac{x^p - 1}{x - 1} = x^{p-1} + \cdots + 1$ is irreducible; for composite $m$, $x^{m-1} + \cdots + 1$ is reducible.

    TMason–Stothers

    If $a(x), b(x), c(x)$ are pairwise coprime polynomials with $a + b + c = 0$, then each has degree $\le N(abc) - 1$, where $N(abc)$ is the number of distinct roots of $a b c$. (Polynomial analogue of the $abc$ conjecture; implies the polynomial Fermat's Last Theorem.)

    Proof idea. Set $p = a/c$, $q = b/c$, so $p + q + 1 = 0$ and $p' = -q'$; compare logarithmic derivatives $\frac{p'}{p} = \sum \frac{a_i}{x - \alpha_i} - \sum \frac{c_i}{x - \gamma_i}$, etc.

    Solving Polynomial Equations by Radicals

    • Linear $ax + b = 0$: $x = -b/a$.
    • Quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$: $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$.
    • Cubic (Cardano): reduce $ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = 0$ to $x^3 + px + q = 0$, substitute $x = t - \frac{p}{3t}$ to get $t^3 + q - \frac{p^3}{27 t^3} = 0$, a quadratic in $t^3$.
    • Quartic (Ferrari): reduce to $x^4 = ax^2 + bx + c$; write $x^4 + mx^2 + n = (a + m)x^2 + bx + (c + n)$ and choose $m, n$ so the right side is a perfect square ($\Delta = b^2 - 4(a+m)(c+n) = 0$), giving $x^2 + \frac{m}{2} = \pm\left(\sqrt{a+m}\,x + \frac{b}{2\sqrt{a+m}}\right)$.

    Modules and Algebras

    A module over a field/ring $R$ is an Abelian group $M$ with a scalar action satisfying distributivity $(r + s)(m + n) = rm + rn + sm + sn$, compatibility $(rs)m = r(sm)$, and unitality $1 \cdot m = m$. An algebra is a module that is also a ring with bilinear (left/right distributive) multiplication.

    Norm

    Vector Norm

    For vector $X\in\mathbb{R}^n$, a vector norm $\left \| X \right \|$ should has the following properties:

    1. $\left \| X \right \|\ge 0$ and $\left \| X \right \|= 0$ iff $X=0$
    2. $\forall \alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, $\left \| \alpha X \right \|=\left | \alpha \right | \left \| X \right \|$
    3. $\left \| X+Y \right \|\le\left \| X \right \|+\left \| Y \right \|$

    A common norm is $L_p$ norm / Hölder norm, which is defined as$$\left \| X \right \|_p=\big(\sum_{i=1}^n \left | x_i \right |^p \big)^{\frac{1}{p}},1\le p\le+\infty$$ The three most commonly used $L_p$ vector norms are

    • $p=1$ (Manhattan Norm) $$\left \| X \right \|_1=\sum_{i=1}^n \left | x_i \right |$$
    • $p=2$ (Euclidean Norm) $$\left \| X \right \|_2=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}$$
    • $p=+\infty$ (Chebyshev Norm) $$\left \| X \right \|_\infty=\lim_{p\to+\infty}\big(\sum_{i=1}^n \left | x_i \right |^p\big)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\max\{\left | x_i \right |\}$$

    Matrix Norm

    For matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, a matrix norm $\left \| A \right \|$ should has the following properties:

    1. $\left \| A \right \|\ge 0$ and $\left \| A \right \|= 0$ iff $A=0$
    2. $\forall \lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, $\left \| \lambda X \right \|=\left | \lambda \right | \left \| X \right \|$
    3. For any two matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same order, $\left \| A+B \right \|\le\left \| A \right \|+\left \| B \right \|$
    4. For any two matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same order, $\left \| AB \right \|\le\left \| A \right \|\left \| B \right \|$
    5. For any vertex $X\in \mathbb{R}^n$, $\left \| AX \right \|\le\left \| A \right \|\left \| X \right \|$

    The matrix norm can be defined as:$$\left \| A \right \|=\sup_{X\in\mathbb{R}^n,X\ne0}\frac{\left \| AX \right \|}{\left \| X \right \|}=\sup_{X\in\mathbb{R}^n,\left \| X \right \|=1}\left \| AX \right \|$$ Correspondingly, the common norm:

    • $p=1$ $$\left \| A \right \|_1=\max_j\sum_{i=1}^n \left | a_{ij} \right |$$
    • $p=2$ $$\left \| A \right \|_2=\sqrt{\lambda_1}$$where $\lambda_1=\max \left | \lambda_i \right |$, $\lambda_i$ is the eigenvalues of $A^{\top}A$
    • $p=+\infty$ $$\left \| A \right \|_\infty=\max_i\sum_{j=1}^n \left | a_{ij} \right |$$
    PProof

    Another common norm is Frobenius norm: $$\left \| A \right \|_F=\big(\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\left| a_{ij} \right |^2\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}$$ For eigenvalues $\lambda$ of the matrix $A$, $$\left | \lambda \right | \left \| X \right \|= \left \| \lambda X \right \|=\left \| A X \right \|\le\left \| A \right \|\left \| X \right \|$$ As a result, $$\left | \lambda \right |\le\left \| A \right \|$$ So we define the spectral radius $\rho (A)$ of the matrix $A$ as $$\rho (A)=\max \left | \lambda_i \right |$$ As a result,$$\left \| A \right \|_2=\sqrt{\rho (A^{\top}A)}$$ A convergent matrix $A$ is defined as $$\lim_{k\to +\infty}A^k=0$$

    TTheorem

    A matrix $A$ is convergent matrix iff $\rho(A)<1$

    PProof
    CCorollary

    A matrix $A$ is convergent matrix iff $\left \| A \right \|<1$

    PProof

    Condition Number

    For a system of equations$$Ax=b$$ If the constant term $b$ has a small disturbance, then$$A(x+\delta x)=b+\delta b$$ Then $$\delta x=A^{-1}(b+\delta b)-x=A^{-1}\delta b$$ Hence,$$\left \| \delta x \right \|=\left \| A^{-1}\delta b \right \|\le\left \| A^{-1} \right \|\left \| \delta b \right \|$$ $$\left \| b \right \|=\left \| Ax \right \|\le \left \| A \right \| \left \| x \right \|$$ $$\frac{\left \| \delta x \right \|}{\left \|x\right \|}\le\left \| A^{-1} \right \|\left \| A \right \|\frac{\left \| \delta b \right \|}{\left \|b\right \|}$$ Define $\left \| A^{-1} \right \|\left \| A \right \|$ as the condition number $Cond(A)$ of the matrix $A$.

    We can find that $$Cond(A)=\left \| A^{-1} \right \|\left \| A \right \|\ge\left \| I \right \|=1$$ If the matrix $A$ has a small disturbance $\delta A$, we can also find that $$\frac{\left \| \delta x \right \|}{\left \|x\right \|}\le\frac{Cond(A)\frac{\left \| \delta A \right \|}{\left \|A\right \|}}{1-Cond(A)\frac{\left \| \delta A \right \|}{\left \|A\right \|}}$$

    The Hierarchy of Rings

    $$\text{Field} \subsetneq \text{ED} \subsetneq \text{PID} \subsetneq \text{UFD} \subsetneq \text{GCD domain} \subsetneq \text{Integral domain}$$ (Euclidean domain $\subsetneq$ principal ideal domain $\subsetneq$ unique factorization domain.) Examples: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian integers, a ED); $\mathbb{Z}\!\left[\frac{1 + \sqrt{-19}}{2}\right]$ (a PID that is not Euclidean); $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (a UFD that is not a PID).

    Selected Exercises

    1. A ring isomorphism $\varphi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the identity ($\varphi^2 = \mathrm{id}$): order is preserved since squares (hence positivity) are preserved, and $\varphi$ fixes $\mathbb{Q}$ densely.
    2. If $x \mapsto x^2$ is a homomorphism on $G$, then $G$ is Abelian: $abab = aabb \Rightarrow ba = ab$.
    3. The Gaussian-type element $\frac{1}{2}(1 + i)$ has infinite multiplicative order (its coordinates satisfy a recurrence that is never $\equiv (1,0) \bmod 5$).
