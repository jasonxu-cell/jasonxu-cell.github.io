    Complex Functions and Differentiability

    Let

    $$ z=x+iy, \qquad \overline z=x-iy, \qquad |z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. $$

    Then

    $$ z\overline z=|z|^2, $$

    and the real and imaginary parts satisfy the useful estimate

    $$ \max\{|\operatorname{Re}z|,|\operatorname{Im}z|\} \le |z| \le |\operatorname{Re}z|+|\operatorname{Im}z|. $$

    Continuity

    A function $f$ is continuous at $z_0$ if

    $$ \lim_{z\to z_0}f(z)=f(z_0). $$

    Complex Derivative

    The complex derivative of $f$ at $z$ is

    $$ f'(z)=\lim_{\Delta z\to 0} \frac{f(z+\Delta z)-f(z)}{\Delta z}, $$

    provided that the limit exists and has the same value for every direction in which $\Delta z$ approaches $0$.

    A function is holomorphic (or analytic) on a domain $D$ if it is complex differentiable at every point of $D$. A point at which $f$ fails to be holomorphic is a singular point.

    Write

    $$ f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y). $$

    If $f'(z)=a+ib$, then for a small increment $\Delta z=\Delta x+i\Delta y$,

    $$ \Delta f=\Delta u+i\Delta v=(a+ib)(\Delta x+i\Delta y) $$

    Equating real and imaginary parts gives the Cauchy-Riemann equations:

    $$ \boxed{ u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x } $$

    and

    $$ f'(z)=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y. $$

    If $u$ and $v$ have continuous first partial derivatives near a point and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations there, then $f=u+iv$ is complex differentiable at that point.

    Harmonic Functions and Harmonic Conjugates

    Differentiating the Cauchy-Riemann equations shows that the real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function are harmonic:

    $$ \Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0, \qquad \Delta v=v_{xx}+v_{yy}=0. $$

    The functions $u$ and $v$ are harmonic conjugates. Their gradients are orthogonal:

    $$ \nabla u\cdot \nabla v=0. $$

    Consequently, if $f'(z)\ne 0$, the level curves

    $$ u(x,y)=c_1,\qquad v(x,y)=c_2 $$

    meet orthogonally.

    Given $u$, a harmonic conjugate can be recovered locally from

    $$ v(x,y)=\int_{(x_0,y_0)}^{(x,y)}(-u_y\,\mathrm{d}x+u_x\,\mathrm{d}y)+C. $$

    Similarly,

    $$ u(x,y)=\int_{(x_0,y_0)}^{(x,y)}(v_y\,\mathrm{d}x-v_x\,\mathrm{d}y)+C. $$

    The path independence of these integrals follows from the harmonicity assumptions on a simply connected domain.

    Elementary Complex Functions

    Exponential, Trigonometric, and Hyperbolic Functions

    TEuler's formula

    $$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$$

    PProof

    We can use Taylor expansion: $$e^{i\theta}=1+i\theta-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}-i\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+...$$ $$\sin\theta=\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+...$$ $$\cos\theta=1-\frac{\theta^2}{2!}+...$$ Substituting the equations, we get the original expression to hold.

    Hence

    $$ \sin z=\frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i}, \qquad \cos z=\frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}{2}. $$

    The real period of $\sin z$ and $\cos z$ is $2\pi$. The hyperbolic functions satisfy

    $$ \sinh z=-i\sin(iz), \qquad \cosh z=\cos(iz), $$

    and have imaginary period $2\pi i$.

    The Complex Logarithm

    To solve $e^w=z$, write $w=u+iv$. Since

    $$ e^w=e^u e^{iv}=z, $$

    we obtain

    $$ u=\ln|z|, \qquad v=\arg z+2k\pi,quad k\in\mathbb Z. $$

    Thus the multivalued logarithm is

    $$ \operatorname{Ln}z =\ln|z|+i(\arg z+2k\pi), \qquad k\in\mathbb Z. $$

    A single-valued branch is obtained by restricting the argument to an interval of length $2\pi$. The principal branch is usually

    $$ \operatorname{Log}z=\ln|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z, \qquad -\pi<\operatorname{Arg}z\le \pi. $$

    Complex Powers

    For $\alpha\in\mathbb C$,

    $$ z^\alpha=\exp(\alpha\operatorname{Ln}z). $$

    Because $\operatorname{Ln}z$ is multivalued, $z^\alpha$ is generally multivalued. If $\alpha=p/q$ is rational in lowest terms, there are at most $q$ distinct values; for irrational or nonreal $\alpha$, there are infinitely many values.

    Roots and Branch Points

    If $z=re^{i\phi}$, its $n$-th roots are

    $$ w_k=r^{1/n}\exp\left(i\frac{\phi+2k\pi}{n}\right), \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1. $$

    The functions $z^{1/n}$ and $\log z$ have branch points at $0$ and $\infty$. A branch cut, often a ray from $0$ to $\infty$, is introduced to prevent a closed loop from changing the value of a chosen branch.

    If $w_k$ is a local single-valued branch of $z^{1/n}$, then

    $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}w_k}{\mathrm{d}z} =\frac{1}{n w_k^{n-1}} =\frac{w_k}{nz}. $$

    Inverse Trigonometric Functions

    Solving the exponential definitions gives the standard multivalued formulas

    $$ \arcsin z=-i\operatorname{Ln}\left(iz+\sqrt{1-z^2}\right), $$

    $$ \arccos z=-i\operatorname{Ln}\left(z+\sqrt{z^2-1}\right), $$

    and

    $$ \arctan z=\frac{1}{2i} \operatorname{Ln}\left(\frac{1+iz}{1-iz}\right). $$

    Particular single-valued inverses require compatible choices of logarithm and square-root branches.

    Cauchy's Theorem and Integral Formula

    Let $C$ be an oriented curve from $z_0$ to $Z$, and let

    $$ z_k=x_k+iy_k,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n,\qquad z_n=Z, $$

    be a partition of $C$. Choose a sample point $\zeta_k$ on each subarc. If the limit exists independently of the partition and sample points, define

    $$ \int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z =\lim_{\max|\Delta z_k|\to0} \sum_{k=1}^n f(\zeta_k)\Delta z_k, \qquad \Delta z_k=z_k-z_{k-1}. $$

    If $f=u+iv$, then

    $$ \int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z =\int_C(u\,\mathrm{d}x-v\,\mathrm{d}y) +i\int_C(v\,\mathrm{d}x+u\,\mathrm{d}y). $$

    For a parametrization $z=z(t)=x(t)+iy(t)$, $a\le t\le b$,

    $$ \boxed{ \int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z =\int_a^b f(z(t))z'(t)\,\mathrm{d}t } $$

    LThe estimation lemma

    If $|f(z)|\le M$ on a curve $C$ of length $L$, then $$ \boxed{ \left|\int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right| \le \int_C|f(z)|\,|\mathrm{d}z| \le ML }$$

    The following theorem is a really important theorem:

    TCauchy's integral theorem

    Let $C$ be a positively oriented simple closed contour bounding a domain $D$. If $f$ is holomorphic on an open set containing $D\cup C$, then$$\int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=0.$$

    PProof

    For $f=u+iv$, Green's theorem gives $$ \begin{aligned} \int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z &=\int_C(u\,\mathrm{d}x-v\,\mathrm{d}y) +i\int_C(v\,\mathrm{d}x+u\,\mathrm{d}y)\\ &=-\iint_D(v_x+u_y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y +i\iint_D(u_x-v_y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\ &=0 \end{aligned}$$ by the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

    One consequence is path independence: in a simply connected domain,

    $$ \int_{z_0}^{z}f(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta $$

    depends only on the endpoints. Therefore

    $$ F(z)=\int_{z_0}^{z}f(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta $$

    is a primitive of $f$ and satisfies $F'(z)=f(z)$.

    TCauchy's integral formula

    If $f$ is holomorphic on and inside a positively oriented closed contour $C$, and $z$ lies inside $C$, then $$f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_C\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta$$

    PProof

    To prove this, choose a small circle $\Gamma:|\zeta-z|=\rho$ inside $C$. By contour deformation,$$\int_C\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=\int_\Gamma\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.$$ Split the latter integral as$$f(z)\int_\Gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{\zeta-z}+\int_\Gamma\frac{f(\zeta)-f(z)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.$$ The first term equals $2\pi i f(z)$. Continuity of $f$ and the estimation lemma show that the second tends to $0$ as $\rho\to0$.

    Holomorphic functions are infinitely differentiable. Differentiating under the integral gives

    $$ \boxed{ f^{(n)}(z)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i} \int_C\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta } $$

    TMorera's theorem

    If $f$ is continuous on a domain $D$ and $$\int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=0$$ for every closed contour $C$ in $D$, then $f$ is holomorphic on $D$. Indeed, the integral from a fixed point to $z$ is path independent and defines a holomorphic primitive $F$ with $F'=f$.

    Thus, on a simply connected domain,

    $$ f\text{ holomorphic} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\text{ continuous and }\int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=0 \text{ for every closed }C. $$

    Power Series and Fundamental Consequences

    For a power series

    $$ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n, $$

    there is a radius of convergence $R\in[0,\infty]$ such that the series converges absolutely for $|z-a|<R$ and diverges for $|z-a|>R$. It converges uniformly on every closed disk $|z-a|\le r<R$.

    The Cauchy-Hadamard formula is

    $$ \boxed{ \frac1R=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_n|^{1/n} } $$

    and, when the ratio limit exists,

    $$ R=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|. $$

    Inside the disk of convergence, a power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term.

    The disk structure follows directly from comparison with a geometric series. If the series converges at $z_0\ne a$, then the terms $a_n(z_0-a)^n$ are bounded, say by $M$. For $|z-a|\le r<|z_0-a|$,

    $$ |a_n(z-a)^n| \le M\left(\frac{r}{|z_0-a|}\right)^n. $$

    The Weierstrass test gives absolute and uniform convergence on $|z-a|\le r$. Conversely, if the series diverges at $z_1$, it cannot converge at any point farther from $a$; otherwise the preceding argument would force convergence at $z_1$.

    More generally, a locally uniformly convergent series of holomorphic functions has a holomorphic sum. Under locally uniform convergence of the derivative series, differentiation can be performed term by term.

    Mean-Value Formula

    If $f$ is holomorphic on $|z-a|\le R$, then with $\zeta=a+Re^{i\theta}$,

    $$ \boxed{ f(a)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+Re^{i\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta } $$

    and therefore

    $$ |f(a)|\le \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f(a+Re^{i\theta})|\,\mathrm{d}\theta. $$

    Maximum Modulus Principle

    If $f$ is nonconstant and holomorphic on a bounded domain $D$, continuous on $\overline D$, then $|f|$ cannot attain its maximum at an interior point. Hence

    $$ \max_{z\in\overline D}|f(z)| =\max_{z\in\partial D}|f(z)|. $$

    If an interior point $z_0$ attained the maximum $M$, the mean-value formula on every sufficiently small circle centered at $z_0$ would force $|f|=M$ on each circle. Overlapping circles then propagate this equality throughout the connected domain, so $f$ would be constant.

    Cauchy Estimates

    If $f$ is holomorphic on $|\zeta-z|\le R$ and

    $$ M(R)=\max_{|\zeta-z|=R}|f(\zeta)|, $$

    then

    $$ \boxed{ |f^{(n)}(z)|\le \frac{n!M(R)}{R^n} } $$

    Liouville's Theorem

    Every bounded entire function is constant. If $|f(z)|\le M$ on $\mathbb C$, the Cauchy estimate gives

    $$ |f'(z)|\le \frac{M}{R}. $$

    Letting $R\to\infty$ yields $f'(z)=0$.

    Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

    Every nonconstant complex polynomial has a zero. Otherwise, for a polynomial $P$, the function $1/P$ would be entire. It tends to $0$ as $|z|\to\infty$ and is bounded on each compact disk, hence bounded on the whole plane. Liouville's theorem would make $1/P$ constant, a contradiction.

    Harmonic Functions and the Poisson Formula

    If $u$ is harmonic on the disk $|z-z_0|<R$, then for $z=z_0+re^{i\phi}$ with $r<R$,

    $$ \boxed{ u(r,\phi)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} u(R,\theta) \frac{R^2-r^2}{R^2-2Rr\cos(\theta-\phi)+r^2} \,\mathrm{d}\theta } $$

    The factor

    $$ P_R(r,\theta-\phi) =\frac{R^2-r^2}{R^2-2Rr\cos(\theta-\phi)+r^2} $$

    is the Poisson kernel. The formula follows by applying Cauchy's formula both at $z$ and at the reflected point with respect to the circle, then taking real parts.

    Taylor and Laurent Series

    Taylor's Theorem

    If $f$ is holomorphic in a disk centered at $a$, then

    $$ \boxed{ f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n, \qquad a_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!} } $$

    throughout every smaller disk contained in the domain of holomorphy.

    Choose a circle $C$ centered at $a$ such that $z$ lies inside it. From Cauchy's formula,

    $$ f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_C\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta. $$

    Since

    $$ \frac1{\zeta-z} =\frac1{\zeta-a} \frac1{1-\frac{z-a}{\zeta-a}} =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(z-a)^n}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}, $$

    the uniformly convergent geometric series can be integrated term by term:

    $$ f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left[ \frac1{2\pi i}\int_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta \right](z-a)^n. $$

    Cauchy's derivative formula identifies the coefficient as $f^{(n)}(a)/n!$. The usual geometric-series bound also shows that the remainder tends to zero uniformly on compact subdisks.

    Laurent's Theorem

    If $f$ is holomorphic in an annulus

    $$ r<|z-a|<R, $$

    then it has a Laurent expansion

    $$ \boxed{ f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n } $$

    with coefficients

    $$ \boxed{ a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta, \qquad n\in\mathbb Z } $$

    where $C$ is any positively oriented circle in the annulus centered at $a$.

    To derive the expansion, place $z$ between two such circles $C'$ and $C''$. The multiply connected version of Cauchy's formula gives

    $$ f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{C''}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta -\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{C'}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta. $$

    On the outer circle,

    $$ \frac1{\zeta-z} =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(z-a)^n}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}. $$

    On the inner circle,

    $$ \frac1{\zeta-z} =-\frac1{z-a} \frac1{1-\frac{\zeta-a}{z-a}} =-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\zeta-a)^m}{(z-a)^{m+1}}. $$

    Together these produce the nonnegative and negative powers in the Laurent series. By contour deformation, the coefficient integral is independent of the particular centered circle chosen in the annulus.

    Uniqueness

    The Laurent expansion is unique. If

    $$ \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}b_n(z-a)^n, $$

    multiply by $(z-a)^{-k-1}$ and integrate around a circle centered at $a$. Since

    $$ \int_C(z-a)^m\,\mathrm{d}z= \begin{cases} 2\pi i,&m=-1,\\ 0,&m\ne-1, \end{cases} $$

    it follows that $a_k=b_k$ for every integer $k$.

    Isolated Singularities

    Suppose $f$ is holomorphic in a punctured disk

    $$ 0<|z-a|<\rho. $$

    Its Laurent series about $a$ is

    $$ f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n. $$

    The terms with $n<0$ form the principal part and classify the isolated singularity.

    Removable Singularity

    The following are equivalent:

    1. $a_n=0$ for every $n<0$.
    2. $\lim_{z\to a}f(z)$ exists and is finite.
    3. $f$ is bounded in some punctured neighborhood of $a$.

    In this case, defining $f(a)=a_0$ makes $f$ holomorphic at $a$.

    For the boundedness criterion, if $|f(z)|\le M$ on $0<|z-a|<\rho$, then on $|\zeta-a|=\rho_0<\rho$,

    $$ |a_n| =\left|\frac1{2\pi i}\int_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-a)^{n+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\right| \le \frac{M}{\rho_0^n}. $$

    For $n<0$, letting $\rho_0\to0$ gives $a_n=0$.

    Pole

    The point $a$ is a pole of order $m$ if

    $$ f(z)=\frac{a_{-m}}{(z-a)^m}+\cdots+ \frac{a_{-1}}{z-a}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n, \qquad a_{-m}\ne0. $$

    Equivalently,

    $$ f(z)=\frac{\phi(z)}{(z-a)^m}, \qquad \phi(a)\ne0, $$

    where $\phi$ is holomorphic near $a$. In the extended complex plane, $f(z)\to\infty$ as $z\to a$.

    Essential Singularity

    If infinitely many negative-power coefficients are nonzero, $a$ is an essential singularity. In this case $\lim_{z\to a}f(z)$ does not exist, even as a point of the Riemann sphere.

    Residues

    For an isolated singularity $a$, the residue of $f$ at $a$ is

    $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Res}(f,a) =\frac1{2\pi i}\int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z =a_{-1} } $$

    where $C$ is a small positively oriented contour around $a$ containing no other singularity.

    Residue Theorem

    If $f$ is holomorphic on and inside a positively oriented contour $C$ except for isolated singularities $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ inside $C$, then

    $$ \boxed{ \int_C f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z =2\pi i\sum_{k=1}^n\operatorname{Res}(f,a_k) } $$

    Residue at a Pole of Order $m$

    If $a$ is a pole of order $m$, then

    $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Res}(f,a) =\frac1{(m-1)!} \lim_{z\to a} \frac{\mathrm{d}^{m-1}}{\mathrm{d}z^{m-1}} \left[(z-a)^m f(z)\right] } $$

    For a simple pole,

    $$ \operatorname{Res}(f,a)=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z). $$

    If

    $$ f(z)=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}, $$

    $P$ and $Q$ are holomorphic near $a$, and

    $$ P(a)\ne0,\qquad Q(a)=0,\qquad Q'(a)\ne0, $$

    then $a$ is a simple pole and

    $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{P}{Q},a\right) =\frac{P(a)}{Q'(a)} } $$

    The Logarithmic Derivative

    If $a$ is a zero of $f$ of order $m$, then locally

    $$ f(z)=(z-a)^m\phi(z), \qquad \phi(a)\ne0, $$

    so

    $$ \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)} =\frac{m}{z-a}+\frac{\phi'(z)}{\phi(z)}. $$

    Therefore $f'/f$ has a simple pole at $a$ with

    $$ \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{f'}f,a\right)=m. $$

    If $b$ is a pole of $f$ of order $n$, then

    $$ \operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{f'}f,b\right)=-n. $$

    Argument Principle and Rouche's Theorem

    Argument Principle

    Suppose $f$ is meromorphic inside and on $C$ and has no zeros or poles on $C$. Let $N$ and $P$ denote, with multiplicity, the numbers of zeros and poles inside $C$. Then

    $$ \boxed{ \frac1{2\pi i}\int_C\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z=N-P } $$

    If the image contour is $w=f(z)=\rho e^{i\theta}$, then

    $$ \frac1{2\pi i}\int_C\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z =\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{f(C)}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{w} =\frac1{2\pi}\Delta_C\arg f(z). $$

    Thus the change of argument of $f$ around $C$ is

    $$ \Delta_C\arg f=2\pi(N-P). $$

    Rouche's Theorem

    Let $f$ and $g$ be holomorphic on and inside a simple closed contour $C$. If

    $$ |g(z)|<|f(z)| \qquad (z\in C), $$

    then $f$ and $f+g$ have the same number of zeros inside $C$, counted with multiplicity.

    Indeed,

    $$ f+g=f\left(1+\frac gf\right). $$

    Because $|g/f|<1$ on $C$, the image of $1+g/f$ lies in the disk $|w-1|<1$, which does not contain the origin. Hence

    $$ \Delta_C\arg\left(1+\frac gf\right)=0, $$

    and $f+g$ and $f$ have the same change of argument.

    Counting Polynomial Zeros from a Change of Argument

    Let

    $$ P(z)=z^n+a_1z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n $$

    have no zeros on the imaginary axis. Suppose that, as $z=iy$ moves from $-i\infty$ to $+i\infty$, the curve $P(iy)$ winds $k$ times about the origin, so that

    $$ \Delta_{y:-\infty\to\infty}\arg P(iy)=2k\pi. $$

    Close the contour by a large semicircle in the left half-plane. On that semicircle,

    $$ P(z)=z^n[1+o(1)], $$

    and the change of argument approaches $n\pi$. If $m$ is the number of zeros in the left half-plane, the argument principle gives

    $$ 2m\pi=2k\pi+n\pi, $$

    so

    $$ \boxed{m=\frac{n+2k}{2}.} $$

    Integral Estimates on Circular Arcs

    Large-Arc Lemma

    Let

    $$ C_R:\ z=Re^{i\theta},\qquad \alpha\le\theta\le\beta, $$

    and suppose $f$ is continuous on $C_R$ for all sufficiently large $R$. If

    $$ \max_{z\in C_R}|zf(z)|\longrightarrow0 \qquad (R\to\infty), $$

    then

    $$ \boxed{ \int_{C_R}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\longrightarrow0. } $$

    Indeed,

    $$ \left|\int_{C_R}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right| \le \frac{\max_{C_R}|zf(z)|}{R}\,R(\beta-\alpha) \longrightarrow0. $$

    In particular, if $f=P/Q$ is rational and

    $$ \deg Q\ge\deg P+2, $$

    then the integral over a fixed-angle arc tends to zero as $R\to\infty$.

    Small-Arc Lemma

    Let

    $$ C_\rho:\ z=a+\rho e^{i\theta}, \qquad \alpha\le\theta\le\beta, $$

    and assume

    $$ \lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)=k. $$

    Then

    $$ \boxed{ \lim_{\rho\to0}\int_{C_\rho}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z =i(\beta-\alpha)k. } $$

    This follows by comparing $f(z)$ with $k/(z-a)$ and using

    $$ \int_{C_\rho}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-a}=i(\beta-\alpha). $$

    For a positively oriented semicircle around a simple pole, the limiting contribution is $i\pi\operatorname{Res}(f,a)$. Reversing the orientation reverses the sign.

    Jordan's Lemma

    Let $g$ be continuous on large upper semicircles $C_R$ and suppose

    $$ M(R)=\max_{z\in C_R}|g(z)|\longrightarrow0. $$

    For every $\lambda>0$,

    $$ \boxed{ \int_{C_R}g(z)e^{i\lambda z}\,\mathrm{d}z\longrightarrow0 \qquad (R\to\infty). } $$

    On $z=Re^{i\theta}$,

    $$ |e^{i\lambda z}|=e^{-\lambda R\sin\theta}. $$

    Using symmetry and $\sin\theta\ge 2\theta/\pi$ for $0\le\theta\le\pi/2$,

    $$ \begin{aligned} \left|\int_{C_R}g(z)e^{i\lambda z}\,\mathrm{d}z\right| &\le 2M(R)R\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-\lambda R\sin\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\\ &\le \frac{\pi M(R)}{\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda R})\longrightarrow0. \end{aligned} $$

    For $e^{-i\lambda z}$ with $\lambda>0$, the analogous contour is closed in the lower half-plane.

    Evaluating Integrals by Residues

    Trigonometric Integrals

    For an integral of the form

    $$ \int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta, $$

    set $z=e^{i\theta}$. Then

    $$ \cos\theta=\frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right), \qquad \sin\theta=\frac1{2i}\left(z-\frac1z\right), \qquad \mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{iz}. $$

    Therefore

    $$ \int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta =\int_{|z|=1} R\left( \frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right), \frac1{2i}\left(z-\frac1z\right) \right)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{iz}, $$

    which is evaluated by summing the residues inside the unit circle.

    Rational Integrals over the Real Axis

    Let

    $$ R(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

    have no real poles and satisfy $\deg Q\ge\deg P+2$. Close the real segment $[-R,R]$ by a large upper semicircle. The large-arc lemma gives

    $$ \boxed{ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)\,\mathrm{d}x =2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a_k>0} \operatorname{Res}(R,a_k). } $$

    Fourier-Type Integrals

    For $m>0$, define

    $$ I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)e^{imx}\,\mathrm{d}x =I_1+iI_2, $$

    where

    $$ I_1=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)\cos(mx)\,\mathrm{d}x, \qquad I_2=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)\sin(mx)\,\mathrm{d}x. $$

    Jordan's lemma permits closing in the upper half-plane. If there are no poles on the real axis,

    $$ \boxed{ I=2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a_k>0} \operatorname{Res}\left(R(z)e^{imz},a_k\right). } $$

    If simple poles $x_1,\ldots,x_\ell$ lie on the real axis and the contour makes clockwise semicircular indentations above them, then the Cauchy principal value satisfies

    $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)e^{imx}\,\mathrm{d}x =2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a_k>0} \operatorname{Res}\left(R(z)e^{imz},a_k\right) +i\pi\sum_{j=1}^{\ell} \operatorname{Res}\left(R(z)e^{imz},x_j\right). } $$

    Keyhole Contours

    Let $0<p<1$ and let $R(z)$ be rational with no poles on the positive real axis. Suppose

    $$ z^{p+1}R(z)\to0 $$

    both as $z\to0$ and as $z\to\infty$ in the slit plane. Choose

    $$ f(z)=z^pR(z), \qquad 0<\arg z<2\pi. $$

    On the upper bank of the positive axis, $z^p=x^p$. On the lower bank, $z^p=e^{2\pi ip}x^p$. The small and large circular contributions vanish, so the keyhole contour gives

    $$ (1-e^{2\pi ip}) \int_0^{\infty}x^pR(x)\,\mathrm{d}x =2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}\left(z^pR(z),a_k\right). $$

    Thus

    $$ \boxed{ \int_0^{\infty}x^pR(x)\,\mathrm{d}x =\frac{2\pi i}{1-e^{2\pi ip}} \sum\operatorname{Res}\left(z^pR(z),a_k\right). } $$

    The sum is over the poles inside the slit-plane contour.

    Identity Theorem and Analytic Continuation

    Identity Theorem

    Let $f$ and $g$ be holomorphic on a domain $D$. If they agree on a sequence of distinct points

    $$ \alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots $$

    with a limit point $a\in D$, then

    $$ \boxed{f(z)=g(z)\quad\text{for all }z\in D.} $$

    Set $F=f-g$. Then $F(\alpha_k)=0$ and continuity gives $F(a)=0$. If $F$ were not identically zero, its zero at $a$ would have finite order, and zeros would be isolated in some punctured neighborhood of $a$. This contradicts $\alpha_k\to a$.

    Analytic Continuation

    Let $f$ be defined on a set $E$, and let $D\supset E$ be a larger domain. A holomorphic function $F$ on $D$ satisfying

    $$ F(z)=f(z),\qquad z\in E, $$

    is an analytic continuation of $f$ to $D$. The identity theorem guarantees uniqueness whenever two continuations overlap on a set having an interior accumulation point.

    Parameter-Dependent Holomorphic Integrals

    Finite Intervals

    Assume:

    1. $f(t,z)$ is continuous in $(t,z)$ for $a\le t\le b$ and $z\in D$.
    2. For each fixed $t$, the function $z\mapsto f(t,z)$ is holomorphic on $D$.

    Then

    $$ F(z)=\int_a^b f(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}t $$

    is holomorphic on $D$, and differentiation under the integral sign is valid:

    $$ \boxed{ F'(z)=\int_a^b\frac{\partial f(t,z)}{\partial z}\,\mathrm{d}t. } $$

    For a contour $C$ surrounding $z$ inside $D$, Cauchy's formula gives

    $$ f(t,z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_C\frac{f(t,\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta. $$

    Integrating with respect to $t$, interchanging the finite integrals, and applying Cauchy's derivative formula proves the result.

    Improper Integrals and Uniform Convergence

    For

    $$ F(z)=\int_a^{\infty}f(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}t, $$

    uniform convergence on $D$ means that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $T(\varepsilon)$ such that

    $$ \left|\int_{T_1}^{T_2}f(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|<\varepsilon $$

    for all $z\in D$ whenever $T_2>T_1>T(\varepsilon)$.

    The Weierstrass test gives a convenient sufficient condition: if

    $$ |f(t,z)|\le\phi(t) $$

    for every $z\in D$, and

    $$ \int_a^{\infty}\phi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty, $$

    then the integral converges absolutely and uniformly on $D$.

    If $f(t,\cdot)$ is holomorphic for every $t$ and the improper integral converges locally uniformly, then

    $$ F(z)=\int_a^{\infty}f(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}t $$

    is holomorphic. Under the corresponding locally uniform hypotheses for the derivative,

    $$ \boxed{ F'(z)=\int_a^{\infty}\frac{\partial f(t,z)}{\partial z}\,\mathrm{d}t. } $$

    One proof chooses

    $$ a=a_0<a_1<a_2<\cdots, \qquad a_n\to\infty, $$

    and writes

    $$ F(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_n(z), \qquad u_n(z)=\int_{a_{n-1}}^{a_n}f(t,z)\,\mathrm{d}t. $$

    The locally uniform convergence theorem for series of holomorphic functions then permits termwise differentiation.

    Gamma and Beta Functions

    Gamma Function

    For $\operatorname{Re}z>0$,

    $$ \boxed{ \Gamma(z)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{z-1}\,\mathrm{d}t. } $$

    This integral is holomorphic in the right half-plane. Integration by parts gives

    $$ \Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z). $$

    Therefore

    $$ \Gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+1)}{z} $$

    continues $\Gamma$ meromorphically to $\operatorname{Re}z>-1$, and repeated use of the recurrence continues it to the whole plane.

    The only singularities are simple poles at

    $$ z=0,-1,-2,\ldots. $$

    Their residues are

    $$ \boxed{ \operatorname{Res}(\Gamma,-n)=\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}, \qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots. } $$

    The reciprocal $1/\Gamma(z)$ is entire, with simple zeros at the nonpositive integers. Thus $\Gamma$, $\tan z$, and $\cot z$ are examples of meromorphic functions.

    Beta Function

    For $\operatorname{Re}p>0$ and $\operatorname{Re}q>0$,

    $$ B(p,q)=\int_0^{\infty} \frac{t^{p-1}}{(1+t)^{p+q}}\,\mathrm{d}t =\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}. $$

    Euler Reflection Formula

    Euler's reflection formula is

    $$ \boxed{ \Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)}. } $$

    For $0<\operatorname{Re}z<1$, the beta integral gives

    $$ \Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z) =\int_0^{\infty}\frac{t^{z-1}}{1+t}\,\mathrm{d}t. $$

    A keyhole-contour evaluation of this integral yields $\pi/\sin(\pi z)$; analytic continuation then proves the identity wherever both sides are defined.

    Conformal Mappings

    Geometric Meaning of the Derivative

    Let $w=f(z)$ map the $z$-plane to the $w$-plane. If a smooth curve is parametrized by

    $$ z=z(t),\qquad z(t_0)=z_0, $$

    then its image is

    $$ w(t)=f(z(t)), $$

    and

    $$ w'(t_0)=f'(z_0)z'(t_0). $$

    Therefore

    $$ \arg f'(z_0)=\arg w'(t_0)-\arg z'(t_0). $$

    If $f'(z_0)\ne0$, multiplication by $f'(z_0)$ rotates every tangent direction by $\arg f'(z_0)$ and scales every infinitesimal length by $|f'(z_0)|$. Thus $f$ preserves oriented angles at $z_0$.

    More precisely,

    $$ f(z_0+\Delta z)-f(z_0) =f'(z_0)\Delta z+o(|\Delta z|), $$

    so

    $$ |\Delta w|\sim |f'(z_0)|\,|\Delta z|. $$

    The factor $|f'(z_0)|$ is the local magnification.

    A Univalent Holomorphic Map Has Nonzero Derivative

    If $f$ is one-to-one and holomorphic on a domain $D$, then

    $$ f'(z)\ne0\qquad(z\in D). $$

    Suppose instead that $f'(z_0)=0$. Since $f$ is nonconstant, there is an integer $m\ge2$ such that

    $$ f(z)-f(z_0)=(z-z_0)^m\phi(z), \qquad \phi(z_0)\ne0. $$

    For sufficiently small $a\ne0$, Rouche's theorem shows that

    $$ f(z)-f(z_0)-a $$

    has $m$ zeros near $z_0$. Hence $f$ takes the value $f(z_0)+a$ at more than one nearby point, contradicting injectivity.

    A one-to-one holomorphic map is therefore conformal everywhere on its domain. Conversely, a holomorphic function is locally conformal precisely where its derivative is nonzero.

    The Riemann Mapping Theorem

    If $D$ is a nonempty simply connected proper subdomain of $\mathbb C$, then there exists a one-to-one holomorphic map from $D$ onto the unit disk.

    With a normalization

    $$ f(z_0)=w_0, \qquad \arg f'(z_0)=\alpha_0, $$

    the conformal map is unique.

    The hypotheses matter:

    • A disconnected set cannot be mapped one-to-one onto the connected disk.
    • The entire plane is excluded; otherwise a bounded entire map would be constant by Liouville's theorem.
    • A punctured plane is not simply connected and cannot be conformally equivalent to a disk.

    Linear Fractional Transformations

    A linear fractional, or Mobius, transformation is

    $$ \boxed{ w=M(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \qquad ad-bc\ne0. } $$

    Its inverse is

    $$ \boxed{ z=M^{-1}(w)=\frac{dw-b}{-cw+a}, } $$

    and

    $$ M'(z)=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^2}\ne0 $$

    wherever $M$ is finite.

    The extended plane $\widehat{\mathbb C}=\mathbb C\cup\{\infty\}$ makes the exceptional values natural:

    $$ M\left(-\frac dc\right)=\infty, \qquad M(\infty)=\frac ac $$

    when $c\ne0$.

    Every Mobius transformation is a composition of the elementary maps

    $$ \begin{array}{ll} \text{translation:} & w=z+b,\\[2mm] \text{rotation:} & w=e^{i\theta}z,\\[2mm] \text{dilation:} & w=rz,\quad r>0,\\[2mm] \text{inversion:} & w=1/z. \end{array} $$

    For $c\ne0$,

    $$ \frac{az+b}{cz+d} =\frac ac+ \frac{bc-ad}{c^2\left(z+d/c\right)}, $$

    which displays this decomposition directly.

    Generalized Circles

    A generalized circle means either a circle or a straight line in $\widehat{\mathbb C}$. Its equation can be written

    $$ A z\overline z+Bz+\overline B\,\overline z+C=0, $$

    where $A,C\in\mathbb R$. Substitution of $z=M^{-1}(w)$ yields another equation of the same form. Hence Mobius transformations map generalized circles to generalized circles.

    Symmetric Points with Respect to a Circle

    For the circle

    $$ C:\ |z-a|=R, $$

    two points $z_1$ and $z_2$ are symmetric with respect to $C$ if they lie on the same ray from $a$ and

    $$ |z_1-a|\,|z_2-a|=R^2. $$

    Equivalently,

    $$ \boxed{ z_1=a+\frac{R^2}{\overline{z_2-a}}. } $$

    Another geometric characterization is that every generalized circle through $z_1$ and $z_2$ meets $C$ orthogonally. Mobius transformations preserve this relation: if $z_1,z_2$ are symmetric with respect to $C$, then $M(z_1),M(z_2)$ are symmetric with respect to $M(C)$.

    Mapping Three Points

    Three distinct points $z_1,z_2,z_3$ can be mapped to three distinct points $w_1,w_2,w_3$ by a unique Mobius transformation. It is determined by preservation of the cross-ratio:

    $$ \boxed{ \frac{w-w_1}{w-w_2} \frac{w_3-w_2}{w_3-w_1} = \frac{z-z_1}{z-z_2} \frac{z_3-z_2}{z_3-z_1}. } $$

    For uniqueness, if two Mobius maps $M_1$ and $M_2$ agree at three distinct points, then $M_2^{-1}\circ M_1$ fixes those three points. A nonidentity Mobius transformation has at most two fixed points, so $M_1=M_2$.

    Standard Mapping Functions

    Powers and Roots

    The map

    $$ w=z^n $$

    multiplies arguments by $n$. Thus the sector

    $$ \alpha<\arg z<\beta $$

    is mapped to

    $$ n\alpha<\arg w<n\beta. $$

    Conversely, if $z=re^{i\phi}$, the branches of $w=z^{1/n}$ are

    $$ w_k=r^{1/n}\exp\left(i\frac{\phi+2k\pi}{n}\right), \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1. $$

    On a sector $\alpha<\arg z<\beta$, the $k$th branch maps into

    $$ \frac{\alpha+2k\pi}{n} <\arg w< \frac{\beta+2k\pi}{n}. $$

    Exponential and Logarithm

    The exponential map

    $$ w=e^z $$

    maps a horizontal strip

    $$ a<\operatorname{Im}z<b, \qquad b-a\le2\pi, $$

    onto the sector

    $$ a<\arg w<b. $$

    The logarithm has values

    $$ w_n=\ln|z|+i(\arg z+2n\pi), \qquad n\in\mathbb Z. $$

    Therefore a sector $a<\arg z<b$ is mapped by the $n$th logarithm branch to the horizontal strip

    $$ 2n\pi+a<\operatorname{Im}w<2n\pi+b. $$

    Half-Plane and Disk

    If $z_0$ lies in the upper half-plane, then

    $$ \boxed{ w=e^{i\theta}\frac{z-z_0}{z-\overline{z_0}} } $$

    maps the upper half-plane conformally onto the unit disk, sends $z_0$ to $0$, and allows an arbitrary final rotation $e^{i\theta}$.

    A standard Cayley transform is

    $$ w=\frac{z-i}{z+i}, $$

    which maps the upper half-plane to the unit disk. Its inverse is

    $$ z=i\frac{1+w}{1-w}. $$

    Compositions with powers, rotations, translations, and inversions give maps between half-planes, disks, sectors, and slit domains.

    Joukowski Transformation

    The Joukowski map is

    $$ \boxed{ w=\frac12\left(z+\frac1z\right). } $$

    Its inverse relation is

    $$ z=w\pm\sqrt{w^2-1}. $$

    The points $z$ and $1/z$ have the same image. Writing

    $$ z=re^{i\phi}, \qquad w=u+iv, $$

    gives

    $$ u=\frac12\left(r+\frac1r\right)\cos\phi, \qquad v=\frac12\left(r-\frac1r\right)\sin\phi. $$

    For fixed $r\ne1$, the circle $|z|=r$ maps to the ellipse

    $$ \boxed{ \frac{u^2}{\frac14(r+r^{-1})^2} + \frac{v^2}{\frac14(r-r^{-1})^2}=1 } $$

    with foci at $w=\pm1$. For fixed $\phi$, a ray maps to a confocal hyperbola:

    $$ \boxed{ \frac{u^2}{\cos^2\phi} -\frac{v^2}{\sin^2\phi}=1. } $$

    The unit circle maps to the line segment $[-1,1]$.

    Schwarz-Christoffel Transformation

    Let the real points

    $$ a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_n $$

    on the boundary of the upper half-plane be mapped to the vertices

    $$ A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n $$

    of a polygon. Suppose the interior angle at $A_k$ is $\alpha_k\pi$. Then

    $$ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_k=n-2. $$

    Near a prevertex $a_k$, the change of direction of the image boundary implies

    $$ \frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}z}\sim C_k(z-a_k)^{\alpha_k-1}. $$

    Combining all prevertices yields

    $$ \boxed{ \frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}z} =C\prod_{k=1}^{n}(z-a_k)^{\alpha_k-1}. } $$

    After integration, the Schwarz-Christoffel map is

    $$ \boxed{ w=A+C\int^z \prod_{k=1}^{n}(\zeta-a_k)^{\alpha_k-1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta. } $$

    The constants $A$ and $C$, together with the prevertices $a_k$, are chosen to place and scale the polygon. Three real degrees of freedom in the prevertices can be fixed by a preliminary Mobius automorphism of the upper half-plane.
